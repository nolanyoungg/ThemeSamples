Make room
for meaningful work.

A better process often begins by taking something away. Thoughts from the fictional Chroma product team.

In this story

Attention is a shared resourceDesign the handoffsKnow what is enough

A productive day and a busy day can look almost identical on a calendar. Both have full blocks, quick conversations, and things checked off. The difference is often what happens in the spaces between them.

Can you find the decision you need? Does the next person understand why the work changed? Is there a clear answer to what matters most? Small moments of clarity have an outsized effect on how a team feels.

Attention is a shared resource.

Every time someone has to reconstruct a project's context, the team pays a small tax. A missing brief becomes a message. An unexplained change becomes a meeting. A half-remembered decision becomes a second round of the same conversation.

The answer is not to document everything. It is to make the important things easy to find: what we are trying to accomplish, what we have decided, and what is still open. A useful record is one someone can understand without its author in the room.

Clarity is not an extra deliverable. It is part of the work itself.

Design the handoffs.

Most teams spend more time designing individual steps than the spaces between them. A brief becomes a design, a design becomes an implementation, and an implementation becomes a release. Each transition is a chance to lose the original intent.

A good handoff carries three things: the purpose, the decisions already made, and the questions that remain. Keep that small package close to the artifact. It should travel with the work, not wait in an unrelated folder.

Know what is enough.

Process is useful until maintaining it becomes the main activity. If a status update takes longer to write than the conversation it replaces, reconsider it. If a field is always empty, remove it. If a ritual has lost its purpose, give it a new one or let it go.

The goal is a team that can make good decisions with less friction. Start small. Notice where the work slows down. Improve that moment, then look again. A thoughtful process grows from the actual work rather than an ideal diagram.

And when a project is finished, leave a little time to name what you learned. A good ending makes the next beginning easier.
